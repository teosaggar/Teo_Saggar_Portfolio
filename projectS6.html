Écologie(s) Pavillonaire

This project takes place in the Marais district of Villepinte, a suburban neighborhood located between the A104 motorway and the wooded grounds of the former sanatorium. The design process began with a territorial survey focused on climate, ecology, and infrastructure. This investigation revealed a landscape shaped by multiple forms of dependency: on technical networks, transportation systems, and surrounding ecosystems. Behind the image of the detached house as an independent unit lies a complex web of relationships between inhabitants, resources, landscapes, and services. Interviews conducted with residents highlighted a paradox. Despite facing issues such as rising energy costs, limited living space, flood-prone soils, and dependence on the car, most inhabitants expressed a strong attachment to their homes. Gardens, DIY construction, domestic improvements, and neighborhood relationships all contribute to a deep sense of belonging. The project therefore emerged from a central question: how can this neighborhood evolve without undermining the qualities that make it meaningful to its residents? Rather than intervening directly within private properties, the proposal works through vacant, abandoned, or underused plots scattered throughout the neighborhood. These sites host a series of collective facilities designed in response to needs identified during the interviews: a workshop, a greenhouse, a shared kitchen, a laundry house, a children’s house, and a workspace. These programs provide new shared amenities while also serving as demonstrations of architectural strategies that could later be adapted to individual homes. The workshop acts as the starting point of the project. Conceived as a place for sharing tools, materials, and practical knowledge, it supports residents in maintaining and improving their homes. It also enables the construction of small-scale interventions such as rainwater harvesting systems and water-management devices. The collective greenhouse extends this approach by creating a space for gardening, learning, and exchange, while experimenting with bioclimatic loggias that can be adapted to existing houses to improve thermal comfort. The different programs are connected through a broader reflection on local resources. Rainwater is collected, stored, and infiltrated through swales and rain gardens; reclaimed materials are reused; tools, equipment, and knowledge are shared. Through these strategies, the project seeks to reduce certain forms of dependency while strengthening social ties within the neighborhood. Rather than proposing a radical transformation of the suburban fabric, the project imagines a gradual evolution based on choice, experimentation, and collective action. By building upon existing practices and forms of appropriation, it explores how individual housing can be complemented by shared spaces, fostering a more resilient and interconnected way of living.
